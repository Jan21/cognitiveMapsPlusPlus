The Imagined Journey

We trained a neural network to estimate travel distances in a little puzzle world. Then we looked inside its head. Three things we found, explained for everyone.

The setup, in one paragraph. The puzzle world is a tiny building of four rooms seen from above. There is a worker, a box the worker can push, doors that can be locked, and wall switches that lock or unlock the doors. The network is shown two photos, "now" and "goal", and must answer one question: how many moves would it take to get from here to there? It answers well, even in buildings it has never seen.

The unusual part is how it answers. The network does not simply output a number. Internally it runs a short "imagination": its picture of the situation moves, step by step, and the total amount of internal movement is the answer it gives. The question of our study: is that imagined journey real reasoning, or meaningless decoration around a lookup table?

1The answer is the length of an imagined trip, and the trip follows the real route

To check the journey is not decoration, we built an identical twin network. Same eyes, same brain, one difference: the twin just blurts out a number at the end instead of measuring its own internal movement. If the journey were meaningless, both twins' internal movements would look the same. They do not.

In our network, internal journey length agrees with true distance almost perfectly (0.90–0.96 on a scale where 1.0 is perfect). In the blurt-a-number twin, the internal movement only loosely tracks distance (0.60–0.67), and the twin is also simply worse at the task.

We then trained a small "mind reader": a simple decoder that looks at the network's internal state mid-thought and guesses where the network currently pictures the worker. The picture it paints: the imagined worker starts at the true start, travels along the actual best route through the building (its imagined position lands on real route squares about 3× more than on random reachable squares), passes through exactly the doorways the real route uses (27× more than the building's other doorways), and drifts toward the goal.

Like being asked "how far is the bakery?" and answering by mentally walking there while counting your steps, rather than just guessing a number.

A charming detail: the imagination keeps an internal price list. An ordinary step of the worker costs a small, remarkably constant amount of imagined effort; pushing the box costs about 5× that; flipping a switch costs 10–17×. The actions that entangle the puzzle's mechanics are billed as the expensive ones.

2The imagination visits the switch exactly when the puzzle needs one, but as a sketch, not a movie

Some puzzles cannot be solved without flipping a switch (the goal is behind a door that is currently locked). Others can be walked directly. We know which is which, so we can ask: does the imagined worker go near switches only when a switch actually matters?

Yes. On puzzles that require a switch, the imagined worker shows up near switches 2 to 4 times more than on equally long puzzles that need no switch. This holds in every difficulty bracket we tested and in every version of the network.

But it is a sketch, not a faithful replay. Real solutions flip the switch early (you must unlock the door before you reach it); the imagined visit happens late or is smeared over the whole journey. And while the imagination knows switches matter, it is only mildly better than chance at picking which of the building's switches is the right one. It also completely ignores the puzzle's other trick (parking the box on a pressure plate): the sketch covers the worker's errand, not every mechanic.

Like planning "I'll have to stop by an ATM somewhere on the way", without deciding which ATM or when.

3A weaker sibling was caught navigating by outdated memories

Our sharpest test secretly rewires familiar buildings: the rooms look identical, but which switch controls which door has been changed. The wiring is invisible in the photos, so a network can only discover it by trial across many examples, or fall back on habit.

Weaker versions of the network (still scoring a respectable ~0.91) sent their imagined worker to the old switches, the ones that used to be correct in training, 19–41× more than to the switches that are correct now. Their final distance answers stayed good anyway. A stronger version (0.96), trained identically except for the random seed, showed no such habit: its imagination went to the currently correct switches.
Like reaching for the light switch where it was in your old apartment. You still find the light eventually; your hand just tells on you.

Two lessons. First, a network can produce correct answers while its internal reasoning runs on stale habit; the output alone would never reveal this. Second, looking at the imagined journey distinguishes two networks that both pass the standard test: one genuinely re-reads the world, the other partly replays what it memorized.

What we are NOT claiming.
  • The network is not a step-by-step planner. Its journey is a rough spatial sketch: right places, wrong order, vague about detail.
  • "Where the network pictures the worker" is our decoder's interpretation of its internal state, and the decoder is approximate (it identifies the right square 50–80% of the time, depending on the network).
  • The old-habit result rests on a small set of specially filtered puzzles and two training runs of the rewired-building network. It is dramatic where it occurs, but we call it a failure mode of weaker networks, not a law.
