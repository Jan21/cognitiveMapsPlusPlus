Two pictures in, one distance out: how the models work and how they score

Every model here sees only two rendered images, the start and the goal state of a switchyard, and must predict the exact number of moves between them. This page explains the current best integrator end to end, the four baselines that consume the very same tokens, and the results across seven levels of environment complexity, on unseen maps and on rewired causal structure.

metric:

1 · THE TASKDistance in a coupled world, from pixels

A 7×7 four-room yard. A worker pushes a crate; three gates sit in doorways and are open or closed; two levers toggle gates (one lever can flip several, XOR-wired); a pressure plate holds its gates open while the crate sits on it; a one-way chute admits the worker from one direction only. A state is (worker cell, crate cell, gate bits). The target is the true BFS shortest path length over the joint state graph. Train on 150 maps, test on 50 held-out maps (map split: new walls, positions, wiring) or on the same 200 layouts with the wiring resampled (wire split: rewired causality).

2 · THE INTEGRATORCurrent best recipe, stage by stage

python3 switchyard.py --train --enc pureimage --cnnk 1 --readout fgpix --coordconv 1 --T 4 --layers 4 --d 128 --heads 4 \
   --cnnw 64 --cnndepth 2 --nmaps 200 --poolq 2000 --steps 80000 --lr 1e-3 --gradclip 1.0 --warmup 2000 --split map|wire
renderstate → image(B, 12, 7, 7)

Everything is drawn, nothing is told

Channels: walls · worker · crate · gate cells · gate-open bits · one channel per lever marking the lever and the gates it toggles (wiring as shared "colour") · plate + the gates it holds · four chute-direction channels. No coordinates, no ids, no adjacency, no wiring table.

coordconvappend x, y(B, 14, 7, 7)

Two coordinate planes

Constant channels holding each pixel's x and y in [−1, 1]. Cheap, and one of the two things that moved the needle: entity tokens then carry an explicit position, not just a learned positional code.

1×1 encoderper-pixel MLP(B, 128, 7, 7)

No spatial mixing

Conv1×1(14→64) → ReLU → Conv1×1(64→128) → ReLU. A 3×3 CNN was the original failure: it blurred which entity lit which cell (gate bits, lever identity) into neighbours, and no amount of slot machinery could recover it (even supervised binding on 3×3 features scored 0.71). Per-pixel features keep every cell's channels exact; the transformer does the spatial reasoning.

fgpix tokenslit cells → tokens(B, 9, 128)

Object tokens without labels

Take the feature vector at every cell that is lit in any entity channel (worker, crate, 3 gates, 2 levers, plate, chute: 9 at full complexity), add the learned position embedding of that cell, pad unlit slots with a learned empty token, add a slot-id embedding. This is an object extractor with zero learning: foreground = non-empty pixel. Which entity a token is comes from its channels; where it is from coordinates + position embedding.

assemble3 copies(B, 27, 128)

Start · goal · anchor

[enc(start)+role₀ ; enc(goal)+role₁ ; enc(start)+role₂]. Only the first block will move; the goal and the start-anchor are re-injected unchanged at every pass ("recall"), so the network never loses its destination or origin.

recall loopT = 4 passesshared 4-layer block

Walk, and pay for the trip

cost = 0
for t in range(4):
    z    = Block(tok)                                  # 4 pre-norm transformer layers, d=128, 4 heads, same weights every pass
    cost = cost + (z[:, :9] − tok[:, :9]).norm(dim=-1).sum(-1)   # L2 length of each state token's move, summed
    tok  = [ z[:, :9] ; goal ; anchor ]               # keep the evolved state, re-inject goal + anchor
D = softplus(scale) · cost                             # one learned positive scalar; that is the whole head

Distance is the accumulated latent path length. The only way to output a large number is to actually move the state tokens far; the model cannot shortcut with a lookup.

trainingAdam 1e-3 · clip 1 · warm-up 2k · 80k

The "safe recipe"

Smooth-L1 to the BFS distance, batch 128, 150 training maps. Learning rate 1e-3 with gradient clipping and a 2k-step warm-up: at 2e-3 without clipping the slot/token read collapsed on some seeds (a token would never bind the worker and training plateaued at MAE ≈ 3). This recipe removed the bimodal seeds.

2½ · THE TOKENS, IN PLAIN WORDSWhat "foreground-pixel tokens + coordinate channels" means

The transformer that does the reasoning does not look at a picture. It looks at a short list of items. Everything in this section is about how the picture becomes that list, without anyone telling the model what a gate or a lever is.

The picture is a stack of stencils

The state is not one RGB image; it is a stack of 12 black-and-white 7×7 stencils, one per kind of thing: one stencil has a dot where the worker is, one where the crate is, one marks the gate cells, one marks which gates are currently open, one stencil per lever marks that lever and the gates it toggles (that is how the wiring is drawn: lever and its gates share a stencil), one marks the plate and the gates it holds open, four mark the chute and the one direction you may enter it from, and one marks the walls. Most cells are empty on every stencil. About 7–9 cells have at least one dot.

Step A: two extra stencils that just say "where am I"

Coordinate channels (coordconv): we add two more 7×7 planes that are the same for every state: one whose value increases left→right (the x coordinate, from −1 to +1) and one that increases top→bottom (the y coordinate). They contain no information about the game; they let every cell "know" its own position as a number, in the same way a sticker on a map might have its grid reference printed on it.

Step B: describe each cell on its own

A tiny neural network (two layers, 1×1 convolutions) is applied to each cell separately. It reads the 12 stencil values plus the 2 coordinates at that cell and writes a 128-number description of that cell. Because it looks at one cell at a time and never at neighbours, the description of a cell cannot be contaminated by what sits next to it: a closed gate stays "closed gate at (3, 5)", it does not blur into "something gate-ish around the middle". (Our earlier 3×3 network did exactly that blurring, and it was the single biggest cause of failure.)

Step C: keep only the cells where something is: one token per lit cell

Before this step we have, for each of the 49 cells, its 128-number description feat[cell] from Step B, and the model owns a learned table of 49 position codes (pimgpos, one 128-vector per cell, learned like any embedding).

  1. Which cells are lit. A cell is lit if any entity stencil (worker, crate, gate cells, gate-open, the lever stencils, plate, chute; walls excluded) has a 1 there. In a full yard that is the worker's cell, the crate's, the 3 gates', the 2 levers', the plate's and the chute's: up to 9 cells, fewer if two things share a cell (worker standing on a lever = one lit cell carrying both dots).
  2. Take their descriptions, drop the rest. Pick feat[cell] for the lit cells only; the other ~40 cells are discarded. 49 vectors become ≤ 9.
  3. Add the cell's position code. Each picked vector gets pimgpos[cell] added (128 + 128 → 128): a token is now "what was drawn on this cell" + "learned code for where this cell is". (Position is also inside feat as plain numbers, via the coordinate channels; the two are complementary.)
  4. Pad to a fixed length. The transformer wants exactly 9 tokens per state. If only 8 cells are lit, the 9th place holds one learned vector emptytok that means "nothing here" (the same vector every time, so the model learns to ignore it).
  5. Tag the places. Each of the 9 places gets a learned code fid[k], k = 0…8, added: only so attention can tell the nine places apart. Lit cells fill the places in random order per example, so place 3 is never "the crate": it is whatever landed there.

That is the whole "object extractor": the fixed rule lit pixel = object, no network deciding what an object is, no threshold to learn, no labels: the equivalent of cutting out every non-blank patch of the stencil sheet. Nothing tells the model which token is a lever or a gate; that identity is implicit in which stencils were lit at that cell (a gate token carries "gate stencil on, gate-open stencil on/off, lever-0 stencil on"), and the model must learn to read it. Its location is present twice: as coordinate numbers inside the description and as the learned position code. So one token ≈ (what was drawn here) + (where here is), for every non-empty cell, in random order, padded to nine: nearly the information the symbolic model receives, but obtained from the picture and per cell rather than per named entity. The reasoning transformer still has to discover on its own that "closed gate whose stencil says lever-1" and "lever-1's cell" belong together.

Why this helps so much

The distance in this world depends on exact facts: is gate 1 closed, which lever opens it, exactly where is that lever. The alternative readout ("slots": a few learned queries that softly attend over all 49 cells) has to find each object by attention, and soft attention smears position: it may report "the lever is somewhere around (4.3, 4.6)". A wrong cell means a wrong detour, and a detour is many moves. Foreground tokens hand over the exact cell; coordinate channels make its position a plain number the network can add and subtract. Together they took the full-switchyard score from 0.80 to 0.875 (error −25%).

What it is not, and the honest caveat

It is not a hand-built object model: nothing says which token is a lever, which gate belongs to which lever, or what any of it means; that must still be learned from the distance signal alone, and the transformer must still associate a closed gate with the lever whose stencil it shares. What is assumed is a property of this rendered world: empty cells are truly empty, so "lit = object" is exact. In natural images that rule would not exist; there the lit-cell step must be replaced by a learned detector or by the slot readout, which is why we also report the pure-slot integrator (16 slots, no rule: 0.843 / 0.852) and are testing hints that only add an "object vs background" feature channel instead of using the rule.

Each baseline's depth × learning rate is tuned on this bed (grid over {2,4,6} × {5e-4, 1e-3, 2e-3}); every bar below is a 3-seed mean at the head's best configuration. Same data, loss, steps, warm-up and clipping as the integrator. The one structural difference is the readout: they compress each state to a vector and compare; the integrator keeps both token sets side by side and lets a recurrent block move one toward the other.

6 · WHAT WE LEARNED

what carried the gains

1×1 encoder (identity per pixel; +0.13 at L2), the safe optimizer (no more bimodal seeds), slots ≥ entities (+0.06 at L3), and finally exact entity tokens + coordinates (fgpix+coordconv: +0.08 corr / −25% MAE at L5). Each was found by a probe that could have killed it.

what did not

3×3 convolution, competitive iterative slot attention (all variants), reconstruction auxiliaries, attention-entropy sharpening, hard attention, slot-overlap penalty, slot LayerNorm, wire-path rendering, more maps, longer training, cosine decay, deeper block, more heads, mixed conv. And the supervised Hungarian binding ceiling on the 1×1 encoder is below the unsupervised model (0.77 vs 0.80–0.88): binding is no longer where the gap is.

status

Baselines are being re-tuned on the fgpix+coordconv tokens (depth × lr, L2/L3/L5) and the full L0–L6 ladders will be regenerated with the new integrator recipe against each head's best representation and configuration, 3 seeds. Sections 4–5 will be replaced by that run; the tables above are the current best evidence, not the final one.
